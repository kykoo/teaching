Accelerometers

Accelerometer: Basic Principle 1

  • As the frame is moved up with acceleration, the weight wants to keep its position
  • As the drum moves with the frame, the pen attached to the weight will draw a downward curve
  • As the frame is moved down with acceleration, the weight keeps it position, the drum moves down with the frame, drawing an upward curve
  • The inertia force \(m \ddot{y}\) balances with the spring force \(k x\), i.e., \(\ddot{y}= \frac{k}{m} x\) where \(x\) is read from the drum.
  • Basically this system cosisting of a frame, a weight and rotating drum, is difficult to be small. We have alternative approaches.
  • However, we need to understand a principle before we proceed, Piezoelectricity.

IEPE Piezoelectric Accelerometer

The equivalent noise acceleration spectral density (in g/√Hz) is

\[ a_{nPE} = \sqrt{4 k_B T \left(\frac{0.01 \omega_0}{mQ} + \frac{\eta C_{PE}}{\omega Q_{PE}^2}\right)} \]

Here the first term is the mechanical-thermal noise; the second term is the electrical-thermal noise of the PE transducer.

  • Probably you don’t understand what this amplitude spectrum means. You can perceive it roughly as “frequency contents of a signal”. You will understand this more clearly after Signal Processing Lectures in the future.
  • Please note that Freq is in log scale, as well as amplitude.
  • The noise level is the most important important performance indicator of an accelerometer commonly represented in g/sq(Hz). Again, you will understand this more clearly after Signal Processing Lectures.
  • This is because extremely small vibration peaks may be hidden by the noise floor
  • Please note that the noise floor is a function of frequency.
  • In lower frequency range close to DC (= 0Hz), the noise gets larger which resulting in an error when integrated twice to get a displacement.
  • This low-frequency noise is universally existing on all types of accelerometers.

Servo Accelerometer

A feedback control system to keep a proof mass at a fixed (null) position. The force required is proportional to the applied acceleration1.

  • Most expensive, top performance
  • Aircraft/tactical for Inertial Navigation System
  • DC sensor

A servo accelerometer uses a closed-loop force-balance: a proof mass is suspended by a spring/damper, its displacement is sensed, and a feedback amplifier drives a coil to apply a counterforce that keeps the mass at the null position. The required drive current (or force) is directly proportional to the applied acceleration, so the output is linear and DC-capable with excellent stability and low noise. This architecture delivers top performance for inertial navigation and aerospace applications, at the cost of higher complexity and price.

Servo Accelerometer: QA750

Servo Accelerometer: QA750

  • Often called as Force-Balance Acc sensor.

The QA750 is a force‑balance (servo) accelerometer: a flexure‑supported proof mass sits between capacitor plates, and its tiny displacement is sensed by the position detector. A feedback amplifier drives the magnet/torquer coil to apply a counterforce that holds the mass at the null position, so the required coil current is proportional to input acceleration along the sensitive axis. The diagram also shows self‑test injection, on‑board regulation, and temperature sensing to stabilize the electronics and improve bias and scale‑factor performance.

Strain Sensors

Vibrating Wire Gauge

\[f = k \sqrt{\varepsilon} \quad k = \frac{L}{2}\sqrt{\frac{A E}{\rho}} \quad \Delta \varepsilon = k \left(f_{1}^{2} - f_{0}^{2}\right)\]

  • Both ends are fixed to a structure, where we want to measure strain between the two fixed points
  • Inside the gauage, there is a steel wire magnetized, with an initial tension. The mounting system has to have a way to control the tension.
  • There are coils above the middle of the steel wire. Thermistor (a temperature sensor) is using 2 wires for temperature compensation. The other two wires are connected to the coils.
  • Then a current pulse is applied to the coil wires, inducing a magnetic field, and then vibration on the steel wire.
  • After that steel wire vibrates freely, as the vibrating wire induces electro-magnetic field, in turn, inducing a current on the coil wires.
  • The current on the coil wire is measured to capture the frequency of vibration of the wire.

Vibrating Wire Gauge

Electrical Resistance Strain Gauge

\[R = \dfrac{\rho L}{A}, \quad \frac{\Delta R}{R} = GF \cdot \varepsilon\]

  • ρ (rho): the material’s electrical resistivity (Ω·m)
  • L, A: Length and Area of the strain gauge
  • R: nominal gauge resistance (e.g., 120 Ω, 350 Ω)
  • ΔR: resistance change (very small)
  • GF: gauge factor (typically ~2.0 for metal-foil gauges)
  • ε: strain (dimensionless; 1000 µε = 0.001)

ERS: How to measure \(\Delta R\)

  • Usually \(\Delta R\) is ~0.1% or less even at high strain = 1,000 \(\mu \varepsilon\)
  • Measuring it with an ohmmeter is impractical
  • Wheatstone Bridge is ideal because it converts resistance change into differential voltage accross D and B
  • Depending on configuration, 1, 2, or 4 ERS are used.

\[\Delta V_{BD} = G (\Delta R_1 - \Delta R_2 + \Delta R_3 - \Delta R_4)\]

Fibre Bragg Grating Strain gauage

How to produce Fibre Bragg Grating:

  • A fiber Bragg grating is typically fabricated by exposing a photosensitive optical fiber (often germanium‑doped) to a spatially periodic UV interference pattern so the core’s refractive index is permanently modulated along a short length. The most common method uses a phase mask: a UV laser illuminates the mask, which creates an interference pattern that writes the grating into the fiber core beneath it. After exposure, the grating reflects a narrow band of wavelengths (the Bragg wavelength) determined by the grating period and effective index; the fiber may then be annealed or recoated to stabilize the inscription.

  • The wavelength of reflected light is depending on the bragg grating period, which is reveals the strain at the point

FBG Wavelength-Division Multiplexing

FBG wavelength‑division multiplexing sends broadband light through a coupler into a single fiber that contains multiple Bragg gratings, each tuned to a distinct wavelength (λ1, λ2, λ3, …). Every grating reflects only its own narrow band, so the returned spectrum shows separated peaks that correspond to each sensor along the fiber. A wavelength interrogator measures the peak shifts, converting them into strain or temperature at each grating location, allowing many sensing points on one fiber with minimal cabling.

Because of the WD-multiplexing, practical number of FBGs can be limited depending on expected range of strains: if two peaks are narrowly spaced, they may meet and make it difficult to distinguish which peak is from which sensor.

BOTDA: Principle

Brillouin Optical Time Domain Analysis

  • Uses stimulated Brillouin scattering: a pump pulse and a counter-propagating probe interact in the fiber; Brillouin gain spectrum (BGS) shifts with strain/temperature.
  • Brillouin frequency shift (BFS) is approximately linear with strain (≈ 0.05–0.06 MHz/με) and temperature (≈ 1 MHz/°C).
  • Spatial resolution set by pump pulse width (e.g., 10 cm – 10 m typical); sensing range up to 10–50 km with repeaters/optimization.
  • Measurement loop: launch pump pulse → sweep probe frequency → measure BGS → fit BFS → convert to strain/temperature (often temperature-compensated).
  • Compared to BOTDR (Brillouin Optical Time‑Domain Reflectometry), BOTDA offers better SNR and resolution but requires access to both fiber ends.

BOTDA Product

  • They claim the technical advances in the spatial resolution, temperature resolution, and the maximum distance to measure (up to 20 km)

Thermistor: Principle

= Temperature-dependent Resistor

Thermistor

  • Semiconductor resistor with strong temperature coefficient (often NTC).
  • High sensitivity in a narrow range (e.g., −40 to 150 °C); great for precision around ambient.
  • Requires linearization and self-heating must be minimized (use low excitation).
  • Alternative for higher accuracy/stability: RTD, Resistance Temperature Detector, made from Platinum (Pt100/Pt1000) with 4-wire measurement.

Thermocouple: Principle

Using Seebeck1 Effect

The Seebeck effect is the generation of a voltage when two dissimilar conductors form a circuit with a temperature difference between their junctions. Charge carriers diffuse from the hot side to the cold side, creating an electric potential that is proportional to the temperature gradient and the materials’ Seebeck coefficients. This is the physical basis of thermocouples, where the measured voltage is used to infer temperature without external power.

  1. Thomas Johann Seebeck, a German physicist, discovered this effect in 1821.
