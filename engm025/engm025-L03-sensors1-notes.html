Accelerometers

Accelerometer: Basic Principle 1

  • As the frame is moved up with acceleration, the weight wants to keep its position
  • As the drum moves with the frame, the pen attached to the weight will draw a downward curve
  • As the frame is moved down with acceleration, the weight keeps it position, the drum moves down with the frame, drawing an upward curve
  • The inertia force \(m \ddot{y}\) balances with the spring force \(k x\), i.e., \(\ddot{y}= \frac{k}{m} x\) where \(x\) is read from the drum.
  • Basically this system cosisting of a frame, a weight and rotating drum, is difficult to be small. We have alternative approaches.
  • However, we need to understand a principle before we proceed, Piezoelectricity.

Piezoelectric Effect 2

  • Mechanical pressure induces a voltage difference
  • Measuring a voltage can measure the pressure (hence force)

Piezoelectric Effect

Why applying a mechanical stress creates a voltage difference?

  • Crystal Structure and Built-in Dipoles

In many piezoelectric materials (e.g., lead titanate, barium titanate, PZT), the crystal has a perovskite structure. In this unit cell, the titanium ion (Ti⁴⁺) sits slightly off-center relative to the surrounding oxygen and other ions. This off-center position creates a dipole moment (tiny separation of positive and negative charge) in each cell — i.e., the cell has an internal polarization even without external forces.

  • Mechanical Stress Changes the Lattice

When you apply mechanical stress (compress, stretch, bend), the entire lattice deforms. At the atomic level, distances between atoms change. Because the Ti⁴⁺ ion is already off-centre, the stress further displaces it relative to the negative oxygen ions in the unit cell. This displacement changes the dipole moment of the cell — increasing or decreasing the net electric polarization. Since the total electric polarization comes from the sum of all tiny dipoles, changing how far the Ti ion shifts changes the electric charge imbalance across the crystal.

  • Why Does the Ion Move?

Mechanically stressing the lattice alters the potential energy landscape of the atoms. Each atom sits in an energy “well” determined by surrounding atoms’ electric and repulsive forces. Deforming the lattice changes these forces. The Ti ion finds a new equilibrium position under the stress because the force balance has changed. This new position is still off-center, but in a different place than before stress, which alters the dipole strength.

In simple terms, Stress physically squeezes or stretches the crystal lattice, which shifts the internal balance of forces on atoms like titanium — so they move slightly to new positions, changing the dipole moment.

This is the fundamental atomic mechanism behind the direct piezoelectric effect: mechanical stress → atomic shifts → change in polarization → measurable voltage or surface charge.

IEPE3 Piezoelectric Accelerometer

It is a very common standard for accelerometers, microphones, and other piezoelectric sensors, in vibration, acoustics, etc.

  • Built-in electroics
  • Powered by a constant current
  • Coaxial cable is for power + signal
  • Moderate cost & performance
  • No DC measurement

Popular companies: PCB Electronics, Endevco

  • The most common piezoelectric accelerometer type
  • Sensor is powered with a constant-current supply over the same coax used for signal (typically 2–20 mA at ~18–30 V).

Inside an IEPE accelerometer, a seismic mass is clamped against a piezoelectric element by a preload bolt inside the metal housing; when the base moves, the mass creates a force on the crystal that generates charge (hence voltage). A miniature built-in amplifier (IEPE electronics) sits near the piezo stack to buffer and convert the high-impedance charge into a low-impedance voltage, which exits through the coaxial connector. The cover and base provide a stiff mechanical path, while the mounting stud fixes the sensor to the structure and completes the force path through the piezo element.

IEPE Piezoelectric Accelerometer

The equivalent noise acceleration spectral density (in g/√Hz) is

\[ a_{nPE} = \sqrt{4 k_B T \left(\frac{0.01 \omega_0}{mQ} + \frac{\eta C_{PE}}{\omega Q_{PE}^2}\right)} \]

Here the first term is the mechanical-thermal noise; the second term is the electrical-thermal noise of the PE transducer.

  • Probably you don’t understand what this amplitude spectrum means. You can perceive it roughly as “frequency contents of a signal”. You will understand this more clearly after Signal Processing Lectures in the future.
  • Please note that Freq is in log scale, as well as amplitude.
  • The noise level is the most important important performance indicator of an accelerometer commonly represented in g/sq(Hz). Again, you will understand this more clearly after Signal Processing Lectures.
  • This is because extremely small vibration peaks may be hidden by the noise floor
  • Please note that the noise floor is a function of frequency.
  • In lower frequency range close to DC (= 0Hz), the noise gets larger which resulting in an error when integrated twice to get a displacement.
  • This low-frequency noise is universally existing on all types of accelerometers.

Servo Accelerometer

A feedback control system to keep a proof mass at a fixed (null) position. The force required is proportional to the applied acceleration1.

  • Most expensive, top performance
  • Aircraft/tactical for Inertial Navigation System
  • DC sensor

A servo accelerometer uses a closed-loop force-balance: a proof mass is suspended by a spring/damper, its displacement is sensed, and a feedback amplifier drives a coil to apply a counterforce that keeps the mass at the null position. The required drive current (or force) is directly proportional to the applied acceleration, so the output is linear and DC-capable with excellent stability and low noise. This architecture delivers top performance for inertial navigation and aerospace applications, at the cost of higher complexity and price.

Servo Accelerometer: QA750

Servo Accelerometer: QA750

  • Often called as Force-Balance Acc sensor.

The QA750 is a force‑balance (servo) accelerometer: a flexure‑supported proof mass sits between capacitor plates, and its tiny displacement is sensed by the position detector. A feedback amplifier drives the magnet/torquer coil to apply a counterforce that holds the mass at the null position, so the required coil current is proportional to input acceleration along the sensitive axis. The diagram also shows self‑test injection, on‑board regulation, and temperature sensing to stabilize the electronics and improve bias and scale‑factor performance.
