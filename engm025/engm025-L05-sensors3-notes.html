How does GNSS determine position?

GNSS measures signal travel time from satellites:

  1. Satellite sends a time-stamped signal
  2. Receiver measures the delay
  3. Distance = (speed of light) × (time delay)
  4. Receiver position found by trilateration

Three satellites enough?

Number of Satellites Required

  • 4 satellites
    • 3 for position (Easting, Northing, Height)
    • 1 to time-difference between Satellites and the GNSS receiver

Accuracy: what affects it?

Main factors that degrade accuracy:

  • Satellite geometry (DOP)
  • Atmosphere (ionosphere, troposphere)
  • Multipath reflections (buildings, bridges), Signal Blockage
  • Receiver + antenna quality

Typical accuracy (rough guide):

  • Smartphone: 1–5 m
  • Survey GNSS (RTK): a few cm

LVDT

LVDT

An LVDT is an electromechanical transducer used to measure linear displacement by converting mechanical motion into an electrical signal. Its operation relies on electromagnetic induction and requires an external excitation source.

The primary coil, located at the centre of the sensor, is supplied with an alternating current (AC) excitation voltage, typically in the range of 1–10 kHz. This excitation generates an alternating magnetic field along the axis of the LVDT. Because the sensor does not generate electrical energy by itself, the LVDT is classified as a passive transducer. The stability of the excitation signal is important, as it directly affects measurement accuracy.

On either side of the primary coil are two identical secondary coils, commonly referred to as S1 and S2. These coils are symmetrically arranged and connected electrically in series opposition. When exposed to the magnetic field produced by the primary coil, voltages are induced in the secondary coils through electromagnetic induction. The output of the LVDT is defined as the difference between the voltages induced in S1 and S2. This differential configuration ensures that the output voltage is zero when the core is in the central (null) position and that the sign of the output voltage indicates the direction of displacement.

The movable ferromagnetic core is positioned concentrically within the coils and is mechanically connected to the object whose displacement is being measured. The core is typically made of soft iron or another high-permeability material and is free to slide axially within the sensor. Its movement alters the distribution of magnetic flux linking the primary and secondary coils, thereby changing the mutual inductance between them. Since the core is not electrically connected to the coils, there is no physical contact or friction, resulting in negligible mechanical wear and excellent long-term repeatability.

The coils are enclosed within a protective housing, which ensures precise alignment and symmetry of the winding arrangement. This structural symmetry is essential for maintaining linearity of the sensor output. The housing also protects the sensor from environmental influences such as dust, moisture, and temperature variations, contributing to the robustness and reliability of LVDTs in field applications.

The output signal of an LVDT is an AC voltage whose amplitude is proportional to the magnitude of displacement from the null position, while its phase relative to the excitation signal indicates the direction of motion. At the null position, equal voltages are induced in both secondary coils, resulting in a net output of zero volts. Within the linear operating range, the output voltage varies linearly with displacement.

In practical applications, the raw AC output from the secondary coils is processed by signal conditioning electronics, which typically include a differential amplifier, a phase-sensitive demodulator, and a low-pass filter. These electronics convert the AC signal into a signed DC voltage that is directly proportional to displacement. This conditioned output is suitable for data acquisition systems, controllers, and structural monitoring applications.
