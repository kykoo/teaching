Leica GNSS Receivers

For the GNSS data, each output file is populated with standard NMEA-0183 $GPGGA (Global Positioning System Fix Data). Leica GNSS receivers output this almost verbatim, with Leica-specific precision.

Latitude / Longitude Conversion (Decimal Degrees)

Given NMEA latitude: 5342.4777474 N

This is in ddmm.mmmmmmm format:

\[ 53^\circ + 42.4777474' \]

Convert minutes to degrees:

\[ 53 + \frac{42.4777474}{60} \approx 53.70796246^\circ \text{ N} \]

Given NMEA longitude: 00026.9948685 W

\[ 0^\circ + \frac{26.9948685}{60} \approx 0.44991448^\circ \]

Since the direction is West, the decimal value is negative:

\[ \approx -0.44991448^\circ \]

Location: Southern England (consistent with UK Leica GNSS observations)

Observations Specific to This File

  • High-precision coordinates (7 decimal places in minutes → millimetre-level resolution)
  • HDOP ≈ 1.2–1.4 → good satellite geometry
  • RTK fixed solution achieved at 15:19:57
  • Stable altitude (≈ 95.7–95.9 m)
  • DGPS correction age ≈ 1 s → healthy correction stream

Overall data quality: excellent GNSS positioning performance.

Converting Latitude / Longitude (Degrees) to Metres

GNSS positions are given in degrees, but engineering applications such as structural health monitoring, displacement tracking, vibration analysis, and drift estimation require distances in metres. Because the Earth is curved, the relationship between angular coordinates and linear distance is not uniform: one degree of latitude is approximately constant, while one degree of longitude depends on latitude.

Conversion Factors

Latitude to metres

\[ 1^\circ \text{ latitude} \approx 111\,320 \text{ m} \]

This value is nearly constant anywhere on Earth.

Longitude to metres

\[ 1^\circ \text{ longitude} \approx 111\,320 \cos(\phi) \text{ m} \]

where ( ) is the latitude in degrees.

Small Change Conversion (Most Common Case)

For small movements (centimetre–metre scale), such as those encountered in RTK GNSS-based displacement monitoring, the conversion can be approximated by

\[ \Delta y = \Delta \phi \times 111\,320 \]

\[ \Delta x = \Delta \lambda \times 111\,320 \cos(\phi) \]

where ( ) is the change in latitude (degrees), ( ) is the change in longitude (degrees), and ( ) is the mean latitude of the site.

Worked Example (UK Latitude)

For a site in the UK, a representative mean latitude is

\[ \phi \approx 53.71^\circ \]

\[ \cos(53.71^\circ) \approx 0.59 \]

This gives the following approximate conversion factors:

Direction Metres per degree
Latitude ≈ 111,320 m
Longitude ≈ 65,700 m

Example GNSS Coordinate Difference

If the latitude change is

Δlat = 0.00000010°

then the north–south displacement is

\[ \Delta y = 0.00000010 \times 111\,320 \approx 0.011 \text{ m} = 11 \text{ mm} \]

If the longitude change is

Δlon = 0.00000010°

then the east–west displacement is

\[ \Delta x = 0.00000010 \times 65\,700 \approx 0.0066 \text{ m} = 6.6 \text{ mm} \]

Engineering Interpretation

At UK latitudes, latitude changes correspond to larger linear distances than longitude changes. Longitude sensitivity decreases as latitude increases due to the cosine term. For RTK GNSS, changes on the order of (10^{-7}) degrees correspond to millimetre- to centimetre-level motion, making this conversion suitable for structural displacement monitoring.

Validity of the Approximation

This linear approximation is valid for relative displacement, short baselines (typically less than 10 km), structural health monitoring applications, and vibration or drift analysis. It is not suitable for long-distance navigation or global-scale mapping, where Earth-centred Earth-fixed (ECEF) coordinates or projected coordinate systems such as UTM should be used.

Summary Formula

\[ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} \Delta x &= \Delta \lambda \cdot 111\,320 \cos(\phi) \\ \Delta y &= \Delta \phi \cdot 111\,320 \end{aligned} } \]
